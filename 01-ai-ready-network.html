What is an AI-ready network? Start with the work it needs to support.

Zero to Market · Markets & Technology · Proposed first article in the AI infrastructure series · Draft for Brittney’s review

An AI pilot can look successful while the people using it are still working around delays, access problems, and unreliable connections. Before buying more infrastructure, it helps to ask a more specific question: what does this application need from the network, and can the network deliver it consistently?

For this guide, an AI-ready network means a network whose connectivity, performance, security controls, and operational visibility have been evaluated against the requirements of particular AI applications. Treat that as a practical working definition. The label alone does not tell you which workloads were tested, at what scale, or against which requirements.

That distinction matters because “AI” can describe very different jobs. A person using a hosted writing assistant, a factory running visual inspection, and a company training a large model do not have the same network requirements.

AI for networking and networking for AI are different questions

AI for networking uses AI to help operate the network, such as interpreting telemetry or assisting troubleshooting. Networking for AI concerns the connections that AI applications depend on. A network can use AI-assisted operations and still be poorly suited to a particular application.

Vendors sometimes discuss these together. Cisco’s AI networking explainer explicitly combines infrastructure supporting AI workloads with AI-driven network operations. When evaluating a proposal, ask which problem the proposed capability actually addresses.

Where does your AI run?

Start by following the application, rather than a product category.

Use caseConnections to examineQuestions to test
Employees using hosted AI applicationsDevice, office or remote connection, internet access, security services, application endpointCan people connect reliably? Where do delays occur? Which data may they submit?
An internal assistant retrieving company informationApplication, model endpoint, document stores, identity services, business systemsCan each user retrieve only authorized information? Which dependency slows the response?
On-site visual inspection or another time-sensitive inference applicationSensors, local compute, operational systems, and any cloud dependenciesHow quickly must a result arrive? What happens when the external connection fails?
Distributed model trainingAccelerators, storage, and the data-center network connecting themHow do congestion and communication time affect job completion and compute utilization?

These are evaluation prompts, not universal specifications. A real-time inspection system may need local processing; a daily batch analysis may tolerate substantial delay.

The specialized demands of distributed training deserve particular care. NVIDIA’s networking material describes high-performance fabrics connecting accelerated compute and emphasizes predictable latency and throughput. Those data-center requirements should not be copied wholesale into the purchasing plan of a business consuming a hosted AI service.

Why bandwidth is only part of the answer

Bandwidth describes how much data a connection can carry. Application performance also depends on delay, variability, loss, congestion, and what happens at the other end of the connection.

A slow response could reflect network delay, model processing, a queue at a provider, a database lookup, or an application making several sequential calls. Upgrading a circuit without locating the constraint can leave the original problem untouched.

A useful evaluation measures the complete user experience and then separates its components. For an interactive assistant, that could include time to the first response, time to completion, failed requests, and performance under concurrent use. The acceptable values should follow the business task. There is no useful single latency target for every AI application.

What makes an AI-ready network secure?

Security starts with understanding who or what is accessing which resources, what information is moving, and where controls are enforced.

NIST’s Zero Trust Architecture rejects implicit trust based solely on network location or asset ownership. Applied to an AI application, that means being inside the company network should not automatically grant access to every document, model, or connected tool.

Network controls are one part of the design. Application permissions, identity, data handling, and model behavior need attention too. A secure connection does not establish that an AI-generated answer is correct or that an agent is authorized to take a particular action.

A practical readiness review

Before requesting an “AI-ready” upgrade, assemble five pieces of evidence:

1. The business task. Identify the users, expected activity, and consequence of delay or failure.

2. The dependency map. Show where data, models, applications, and supporting services sit, including external providers.

3. The performance baseline. Measure the current experience under representative load and investigate the largest constraints.

4. The access and recovery design. Document permissions, data boundaries, failure behavior, and who can stop or restore the service.

5. The acceptance test. Define what must improve, how it will be measured, and who owns the decision to proceed.

This is a proposed planning checklist, not a certification or a substitute for architecture validation. Its purpose is to make a broad readiness claim testable.

Common questions

Does using cloud AI mean the network is somebody else’s problem?

No. The provider operates its infrastructure, but your users still depend on access connections, security controls, and any systems integrated with the application. Map the shared dependencies before assigning responsibility.

Does every business need a new network for AI?

No. Establish the workload requirements and test the existing environment first. The gap may involve configuration, access policies, application design, or a specific connection rather than a full replacement.

Does AI readiness require an autonomous network?

Not by this guide’s working definition. Automation may help operations, but readiness needs evidence that the network supports the intended workload. The presence of an AI feature is not that evidence.

How does this relate to post-quantum readiness?

They are separate planning questions. AI readiness evaluates support for AI applications; post-quantum planning concerns the transition of cryptographic protections. One label does not establish the other. A later article can examine where the programs intersect.

What should a buyer ask a vendor?

“Which workload did you test, under what conditions, what improved, and what remains our responsibility?” Ask for the evidence behind the answer, including dependencies and failure scenarios.

The useful outcome is a concrete statement: this application can serve these users, at this level of demand, within these performance and security requirements. That gives both buyer and supplier something more valuable than a readiness label.
